I have spent most of my career learning things the hard way.

I did not study Computer Science. I started in support, learned by breaking things, asking questions, reading documentation and building increasingly ridiculous setups at home. That path worked for me, but it also meant spending a lot of time wondering if I was learning the right thing, moving in the right direction or simply getting better at digging holes for myself.

Being self-taught is often presented like some heroic story. Look at this person, they figured everything out alone! What we leave out is the amount of wasted time, self-doubt and bad decisions hidden inside the word "alone."

Nobody builds a meaningful career alone. At some point, someone answers your dumb question without making you feel dumb. Someone reviews your work and tells you the truth. Someone recommends you for an opportunity you would not have applied for because you were convinced you were not ready.

That person may not call themselves a mentor, but that is what they are doing.

## A mentor gives you perspective

Early in your career, almost every decision feels bigger than it is.

Should I learn Python or Go? Should I move into cloud? Should I become an SRE? Is Kubernetes worth learning? Am I underpaid? Am I falling behind because someone on Twitter built a database from scratch over the weekend while I spent mine trying to understand why DNS was broken?

You can search for answers, watch videos and ask the internet. You will get 400 opinions, three affiliate links and at least one person telling you to learn Rust.

A mentor knows enough about you to give the answer context. They can tell the difference between something that is useful for your career and something you are chasing because it is popular this week. More importantly, they can see patterns that are almost impossible to notice while you are living through them.

Sometimes you do not need another certification or side project. You need somebody to tell you, "You already know enough. Apply for the job."

I think that is one of the most powerful things a mentor can do: lend you some of their confidence until you build your own.

## Good mentorship is not constant praise

A mentor is not supposed to tell you that every idea is amazing.

The people who help us grow are often the ones willing to say the uncomfortable thing with good intentions. Maybe you are avoiding a skill because it scares you. Maybe your communication is holding you back. Maybe you keep blaming the company when you have not been clear about what you want. Maybe you are technically ready for the next level but nobody knows because you never speak about your work.

That feedback can hurt. Nobody enjoys finding out that the problem is, at least partially, themselves.

But honest feedback from someone who wants you to succeed feels different from criticism meant to make you feel small. One closes a door. The other points at the door and tells you where the handle is.

Good mentors challenge you without humiliating you. They do not solve every problem for you either. They ask questions, make you explain your reasoning and let you struggle enough to learn without allowing you to drown.

I have tried to bring that into the way I help newer engineers. I will not immediately give someone a command to copy and paste if I think they can find the answer. I would rather help them build the troubleshooting habits that will still be useful after that command changes.

That may occasionally make me look like an asshole. Hopefully a useful asshole.

## Your manager is not automatically your mentor

A great manager can absolutely become a mentor, but the roles are different.

Your manager has responsibilities to the company, the team and your performance. A mentor can focus more freely on you. They can tell you that the best career move might be on another team or even at another company. That advice is harder to expect from the person responsible for keeping their team staffed.

Mentors can also come from unexpected places:

- A senior engineer who takes time to explain why, not only how
- A coworker at the same level who is stronger in an area you want to learn
- Someone from another team who understands a career path you are considering
- A former manager who continues to care after the reporting relationship ends
- A person outside your company who has already made the mistakes you are about to make

You also do not need one magical mentor for everything. The person who helps you become a better engineer may not be the right person to advise you about leadership, public speaking or negotiating an offer.

Careers are long. Different people will help during different chapters.

## What happens when you do not have one

I wrote before about feeling stuck and directionless at work. I had spent months doing repetitive work, learning very little and slowly losing the excitement that brought me into technology in the first place.

During that period, I realized I had not had a mentor in years.

I still had smart coworkers. I still had friends I could talk to. I could still learn a new tool in the homelab whenever I wanted. What I did not have was someone looking at the bigger picture with me and asking the questions I was avoiding.

What do you want next?

Why are you still here if you are unhappy?

What are you afraid will happen if you leave?

Are you stuck because there is no path forward, or because taking the next step is scary?

Those questions sound obvious when written down. They do not feel obvious when you have a family, bills, responsibilities and a career that looks perfectly fine from the outside. Stability can make you grateful and miserable at the same time. That is a difficult thing to admit, even to yourself.

A mentor cannot make the decision for you. They can make sure fear is not making it alone.

## Finding a mentor without making it weird

Asking a stranger, "Will you be my mentor?" can feel like proposing marriage before the first date.

Start smaller.

Find someone whose work, judgment or career you respect. Ask a specific question. Share enough context for them to give a useful answer. If the conversation helps, follow up later and tell them what you did with the advice.

Mentorship often grows from repeated conversations, not a formal agreement with a monthly calendar invite and a five-page career framework.

Also, do some work before asking for help. A good mentor can guide you, but they should not care about your career more than you do. Bring questions. Try things. Be honest when you ignored their advice. Respect their time.

And please do not only approach people when you need a referral.

The relationship should have some humanity in it. Mentors are not career vending machines where you insert a coffee and receive a promotion.

## Be the person you needed

At some point, if you are lucky, people start coming to you for advice.

This can be terrifying because you still remember being the person who knew nothing. The secret is that feeling never completely disappears. You can have years of experience and still spend an afternoon losing an argument with a YAML file.

You do not need to know everything to mentor someone. You need to listen, care and be honest about what you know and what you do not.

Sometimes mentorship is reviewing a resume. Sometimes it is explaining a technical concept for the fourth time without getting annoyed. Sometimes it is recommending someone for a project because you know they are ready, even if they do not know it yet.

And sometimes it is checking on a person who has gone quiet.

That last one matters more than we admit. People rarely announce that they are losing confidence or thinking about leaving the industry. They withdraw. They stop asking questions. They do the minimum because trying has started to hurt.

A simple "How are you doing, really?" can arrive at exactly the right time.

## Careers are made of people

We spend a lot of time in tech talking about skills. Learn cloud, containers, networking, a programming language and whatever framework became mandatory while you were sleeping.

Skills matter. They get you through interviews and help you do the job.

But when I look at the moments that change a career, there is usually another person somewhere in the story. Someone gave advice, opened a door, offered honest feedback or believed in us before we had enough evidence to believe in ourselves.

If you have that person, tell them what their help meant. They may have no idea how much one conversation changed for you.

If you do not have one, start looking. Do not wait until you are completely burned out or directionless.

And if you are far enough in your career to help somebody else, look around. There is probably a person near you trying to appear confident while quietly wondering if they belong.

You do not have to map their entire future. Help them see the next step.

That may be enough to change everything.
